What it does

What is bccsrtool?

bccsrtool.ca uses BC's Contaminated Sites Regulation (CSR) Schedule 3.1 to screen whether a site needs remediation. It covers roughly 40 chemical compounds most likely found at BC industrial sites, currently screening for human health protection with environmental protection coming soon.

Phase I Site review
Phase II Soil sampling
Phase III Active cleanup
Why I built this

Make the screening logic easier to see.

I built this project to make contaminated sites screening easier to understand. Instead of reading threshold tables in isolation, users can walk through the same logic used in an early environmental assessment: land use, contaminants of concern, lab results, and screening outcomes.

Workflow

Four decisions, one screening path

Each step mirrors a small part of how early ESA information gets organized.

01

Land Use

Different land uses carry different risk assumptions. A residential site usually has stricter limits than an industrial property because people may live there long-term.

02

Contaminants

Site history points toward likely contaminants. A former gas station suggests BTEX and petroleum VOCs; a dry cleaner suggests chlorinated solvents.

03

Lab Data

Measured concentrations are entered with units. Non-detects can be marked when a lab did not detect a substance above its reporting limit.

04

Screening Results

The tool compares the value against the selected threshold and flags below, borderline, exceedance, non-detect, or review.

Site history clues

Site history gives the contaminant list a starting point

Phase I observations rarely give a final answer, but they help narrow the first screening list.

Fuel Former gas station

BTEX and petroleum VOCs often become the first substances to check.

Why it matters: fuel releases can affect soil, groundwater, and vapour pathways.
Solvent Former dry cleaner

PCE/TCE chlorinated solvents are common screening candidates.

Why it matters: solvents can be mobile and persistent.
Rail Rail yard or wood treatment

PAHs, metals, and creosote-related contaminants may be relevant.

Why it matters: historical handling areas can leave mixed impacts.
Fill Fill material or industrial land

Metals and miscellaneous regulated substances are often worth reviewing.

Why it matters: imported fill can carry a different site history.
Method

Threshold logic, made visible

Different land uses, matrices, and units change which Schedule 3.1 standard is used.

Proposed land use selects the standard.

The same concentration can screen differently under residential, commercial, or industrial assumptions.

Units point to the matrix.

Soil units compare against soil thresholds. Water units compare against groundwater thresholds.

Near-threshold values deserve attention.

Borderline means the value is close to the threshold and should be reviewed carefully.

Non-detect does not mean zero.

It means the lab did not detect the substance above its reporting limit. In this educational screen, non-detects are not treated as exceedances.

Responsible use

Simplify early screening decisions.

Educational simplifications

  • Not all regulated substances or exposure pathways are included.
  • Background concentrations are not evaluated.
  • Pathway applicability is intentionally simplified.

Regulatory limitations

  • Thresholds must be verified against official regulations.
  • Schedule 3.1 is the current focus; more standards may be added later.
  • This is not a replacement for a qualified professional or Approved Professional review.
